## Why They Are Still Called Woods

Fairway woods kept their original name even after the materials changed. Earlier heads were made from wood, including persimmon and laminated maple. Modern versions generally use hollow metal or composite construction. The name now describes a family of clubs rather than what every head is made from. This is a useful reminder that golf equipment often carries its history in its vocabulary. [1]

## The Move to Metal

TaylorMade introduced its Pittsburgh Persimmon metalwood in 1979, an important milestone in the commercial growth of metal-headed clubs. It was part of a broader change in woods, rather than the invention of every modern fairway-wood feature at once. Over time, manufacturers explored different head shapes, face designs, and ways to distribute weight. [2]

### A Familiar Job with New Tools

The fairway wood's basic purpose stayed recognizable: provide a longer shot while remaining usable from the ground. That combination separates it from a club designed mainly around hitting a teed ball.

For a visitor comparing old and new clubs, three questions make the history easier to follow:

- What material forms the head?
- How is the head shaped for contact with the ground?
- What kind of shot was the player trying to produce?

These questions connect the original wooden clubs to later engineering changes without assuming that newer equipment automatically produces a better shot for everyone.
